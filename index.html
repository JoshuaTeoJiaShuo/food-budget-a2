CONCLUSION

A bigger bill isn’t the whole story

More of the budget went out the door

Meals out grew from 24.5% of food spending in 1988–89 to 33.9% in 2015–16. In the final survey, it was the largest detailed category at $80.27 a week. That is a shift in where food dollars went, not a count of meals eaten.

A smaller receipt can take a bigger bite

The lowest-income group spent $144.40 a week on food, against $339.32 in the highest group. Yet food took 18.7% versus 14.3% of total goods-and-services spending. A smaller dollar bill did not mean a smaller budget share.

The price story depends on the food, and the shop

Fruit and vegetable annual price changes ranged from an 8.3% fall to a 16.1% rise in 2016–26. In the separate NT survey in 2023, all 54 remote stores priced both standard baskets above their district supermarket. National price movements and local checkout prices answer different questions.

Spending tells only part of the story

Foodbank’s 2025 survey estimated 33% of households faced moderate or severe food insecurity. The estimate reached 68% among single-parent households, compared with 28% without children. Having enough food is a different outcome from spending more or less on it.

The divide is about the slice of the budget, the prices at the checkout, and access to enough food.

These datasets cover different years and populations. They do not follow the same households or establish a causal chain.

Income groups: sources and definitions
About the income groups and these two measures

Both charts use the same five equivalised disposable household income quintiles, ordered from lowest to highest. Equivalised income adjusts for household size and composition. The dollar amounts remain expenditure per household, not per person.

Other food purchases equal total food and non-alcoholic beverage expenditure minus meals out and fast foods. This includes prepared foods and should not be interpreted solely as ingredients for home cooking.

Budget shares are the percentages published by ABS. Their denominator is total expenditure on goods and services, not income. They have not been recalculated from the displayed dollar estimates, which can differ slightly because of rounding and confidentiality adjustments.

The 4.6 and 1.7 ratios compare the highest-income group's spending with the lowest-income group's spending for each component. The 4.4 percentage-point gap is 18.7 minus 14.3.

These are group-level estimates from one survey, not changes experienced by the same households. Differences have not been tested for statistical significance. Neither measure directly establishes financial stress or food insecurity.

Import data and map notes

ABS Table 18.7 provides customs import values in Australian dollars, millions, for December 2025. This single-month snapshot does not describe annual patterns or the latest available month.

The six largest published values were selected within each category. Missing or confidential values were not converted to zero. Import values are not retail prices, household expenditure or physical quantities. Without domestic production, they cannot establish the imported share of Australia's food supply.

Trade categories do not exactly match household expenditure or CPI categories. These datasets do not establish what caused consumer prices to change.

Line widths use one scale across categories. Neutral marks show all origins; the dark highlight identifies the selected origin in both views. Marker size encodes no quantity.

Full origin names appear in tooltips. Grouped origins use an anchor in their principal named country. Anchors were derived from the basemap, except Singapore's approximate marker, and do not identify ports.

Consumer price data and calculations

Each observation compares a category's quarterly CPI with the corresponding quarter one year earlier: 100 × (current index ÷ year-earlier index − 1). Connecting areas interpolate between quarterly observations.

The indexes represent the weighted average of Australia's eight capital cities. They do not represent every town or each household's particular basket.

A higher inflation rate does not mean a category has the highest dollar price. “Food products n.e.c.” means food products not elsewhere classified.

This series does not follow the households in the 2015–16 expenditure survey or establish how their spending or food security subsequently changed.

Northern Territory baskets and store comparisons

The Healthy Food Basket is based on the Australian Dietary Guidelines. The Current Diet Basket reflects dietary patterns of Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander people from survey data.

The extract uses the “Deidentified stores combined” sheet. It contains 54 remote stores: 3 in Barkly, 11 in Big Rivers, 20 in Central Australia, 10 in East Arnhem and 10 in Top End. Each district has one supermarket comparison. District-average rows are excluded.

Remote-store data were collected in July–August and November–December 2023, rather than on a single day. Equivalent brands or pack sizes may have been substituted when identical items were unavailable.

Store names are withheld. Reference labels identify extract rows, not businesses. Tooltips show costs to the nearest cent. Each store retains the same vertical offset in both panels; these offsets have no numerical meaning.

The comparisons do not isolate the effects of transport, competition or other possible causes of price differences.

Food insecurity: sources and definitions

Source: Foodbank Hunger Report 2025 . National categories: Figure 4; state estimates: Table 11; parental-status estimates and sample sizes: report tables. Map boundaries: ABS Australian Statistical Geography Standard, July 2016 , simplified for display.

Survey coverage and food-security categories

Foodbank/Ipsos surveyed 4,260 respondents online using quotas and weighting. Fieldwork ran from 24 June to 17 July 2025. These are weighted survey estimates, rather than a census of Australian households.

The 100 house symbols represent rounded published percentages, not individual households or respondents.

This survey uses a different sample and measure from the earlier ABS expenditure survey. It does not establish that households spending less were food insecure or that the price changes shown earlier caused these outcomes. The 2025 findings also precede the June 2026 endpoint of the price chart.

Interpreting state and household comparisons

All comparisons use the published percentage experiencing moderate or severe food insecurity. Sample sizes count respondents, not affected households. Land area does not represent population, so a larger coloured region need not contain more food-insecure households.

Generalised 2016 boundaries provide geographical context for the 2025 estimates. Matching state names with the earlier spending maps does not establish a causal relationship between spending and food insecurity.

Parental-status sample sizes were 130 single-parent respondents, 1,080 non-single-parent respondents, 133 relatives or co-residents of a child, and 2,917 respondents in households without children.

The national benchmark includes these groups; it is not an independent control group. Connecting lines show percentage-point differences, not confidence intervals or statistical significance. In particular, the 39% and 40% estimates should not be interpreted as a reliable ranking.

These parental-status groups are not the same households observed in the earlier ABS survey. The comparison describes an association with household circumstances, rather than establishing causes of food insecurity.
