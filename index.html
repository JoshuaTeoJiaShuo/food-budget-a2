01 / THE NATIONAL BASKET

Meals out took a growing share of the food budget

Between 1988–89 and 2015–16, meals out and fast foods rose from about a quarter to a third of Australian household food spending. They were the largest food category in all six surveys shown.

By 2015–16, the remaining two-thirds of food spending covered a more fragmented basket of purchases. Looking inside those broad groups reveals how households divided their weekly bill.

That $80.27 meals-out bill was a national average. Across states and territories, the estimates ranged from $59.86 in Tasmania to $100.82 in the ACT.

Geography reveals one kind of variation. State averages still combine households of different sizes and circumstances—the next comparison looks at the households behind those averages.

02 / BEHIND THE AVERAGE

Different households divided their food budgets differently

Group households devoted 54.5% of their food spending to meals out and fast foods. Yet they accounted for only 3.4% of estimated total food expenditure across the seven household types.

Couples with dependent children contributed the largest share of total food spending, 38.3%, while allocating 32.4% of their own food expenditure to meals out. A group’s contribution to the national bill and its own spending pattern therefore describe different things.

The green portions combine many other purchases. Separating those categories reveals another consideration: a household bill may be shared by one person or several. Switching the comparison below shows how the pattern changes when household size is taken into account.

Household size changes the meaning of a dollar comparison. Income adds another dimension: how much of the household’s total spending does food absorb?

03 / THE FOOD BUDGET DIVIDE

Smaller food bills. Larger budget shares.

In 2015–16, the lowest-income group spent $144.40 a week on food, compared with $339.32 in the highest-income group. Yet food occupied a larger share of the lower-income group's total spending.

AUSTRALIA · 2015–16 · FIVE INCOME GROUPS

A smaller food bill does not necessarily indicate a smaller claim on the household budget.

About the income groups and these two measures

Both charts use the same five equivalised disposable household income quintiles, ordered from lowest to highest. Equivalised income adjusts for household size and composition. The dollar amounts remain expenditure per household, not per person.

Other food purchases equal total food and non-alcoholic beverage expenditure minus meals out and fast foods. This includes prepared foods and should not be interpreted solely as ingredients for home cooking.

Budget shares are the percentages published by ABS. Their denominator is total expenditure on goods and services, not income. They have not been recalculated from the displayed dollar estimates, which can differ slightly because of rounding and confidentiality adjustments.

The 4.6 and 1.7 ratios compare the highest-income group's spending with the lowest-income group's spending for each component. The 4.4 percentage-point gap is 18.7 minus 14.3.

These are group-level estimates from one survey, not changes experienced by the same households. Differences have not been tested for statistical significance. Neither measure directly establishes financial stress or food insecurity.

Source: ABS Household Expenditure Survey, 2015–16 . Weekly amounts are in 2015–16 Australian dollars. Food shares use total goods and services expenditure.

04 / THE COST OF FOOD

The food bill also depends on the prices households face

Spending differences reflect both what households purchase and what those purchases cost. Australia's food supply includes overseas sources, while consumer prices vary over time and basket costs differ between places.

These trade connections describe part of the supply system. They do not establish why retail prices changed. For the price movements experienced by consumers, we turn to the Consumer Price Index.

These indexes describe price changes across Australia's eight capital cities. They do not show the dollar cost of a particular basket in every community. A separate Northern Territory survey provides a closer look at differences between stores.

A basket's price is only one side of affordability. The other is the household's resources. Foodbank's separate 2025 survey brings the story back to whether households had enough to eat.

Import data and map notes

ABS Table 18.7 provides customs import values in Australian dollars, millions, for December 2025. This single-month snapshot does not describe annual patterns or the latest available month.

The six largest published values were selected within each category. Missing or confidential values were not converted to zero. Import values are not retail prices, household expenditure or physical quantities. Without domestic production, they cannot establish the imported share of Australia's food supply.

Trade categories do not exactly match household expenditure or CPI categories. These datasets do not establish what caused consumer prices to change.

Consumer price data and calculations

Each observation compares a category's quarterly CPI with the corresponding quarter one year earlier: 100 × (current index ÷ year-earlier index − 1). Connecting areas interpolate between quarterly observations.

The indexes represent the weighted average of Australia's eight capital cities. They do not represent every town or each household's particular basket.

A higher inflation rate does not mean a category has the highest dollar price. “Food products n.e.c.” means food products not elsewhere classified.

THE FOOD BUDGET DIVIDE

The average food bill hides unequal circumstances

Meals out took a growing share of Australia's food budget, while spending patterns differed across household types and income groups. Household size also changed the picture: a larger household bill did not necessarily mean more spending per person.

In 2015–16, lower-income households spent fewer food dollars but allocated a larger share of their total expenditure to food. Foodbank's separate 2025 survey found that food insecurity also varied across household circumstances, with single-parent households reporting the highest estimate among the parental-status groups shown.

Understanding Australia's food budget requires looking beyond dollars spent to the share of the budget food absorbs—and whether households have enough to eat.

These sources describe different periods, measures and survey samples. They provide complementary perspectives; they do not track the same households or establish that earlier spending patterns caused later food insecurity.
